TÜRKÇE

Room Four

story twenty-three

Listen · 9:40

The Widow is here for four months of her husband and everybody at this table knows it, and not one of them will say so before the cards are out.

Room Four is used for viewings in the day and for this at night, and the house takes five per cent of every pot to the second decimal, which it rounds down, which is the only generosity in the building.

The room smells of the lots. There is a rail along the back wall where the coats go and a green cloth laid over the valuing table, and under the cloth the table is still the table, so a man who leans on the edge feels the drawer runners against his forearm and remembers where he is.

Five players. The dealer is house and says four things all night: Action on you. Time. Pot’s right. Cards up.

The blinds are fifty and a hundred. It is a quarter past one.

THE VALUER has the button and eleven thousand in front of him in the good chips. He works upstairs in daylight and has valued three of the four coats on the rail. He plays sevens, sixes and fives all night and never once shows them.

THE STEWARD sits under the light with his gray coat over the back of the chair, which is a thing he does not do at work. He is the big blind. Four thousand two hundred. He has been at this table nine years and has never raised the stakes and has never missed a Thursday.

THE BOY is twenty-three and has seven thousand two hundred, most of it somebody else’s by morning, and a band on his wrist that has been bright since eleven o’clock. He is up from the Tank, where he was moved towards somebody all evening and did not go.

THE WIDOW is the small blind tonight and has eight thousand six hundred, counted twice. She needs four months of renewals and the month is the ninth. She has been playing here since her husband died and the room has stopped calling her the widow and calls her nothing at all, which she prefers.

THE FOREMAN is off the far hill with site money, four thousand seven hundred of it still in front of him, and a drink he keeps turning without lifting. His hands are wrecked. He has bet on what the works are for and lost twice.

The cards go out.

The Boy looks at his once and puts them face down and touches them twice, which the Steward sees and files, along with the fact that the Boy has done this every time he has liked his hand and has not done it the four times he has bluffed. It is twenty past one and the Boy does not know this about himself.

The Widow does not look at all. She lifts a corner at the last moment, from the felt, and puts them down flat. She has a way of touching the chips that is exactly the way she touches a folder, which is to say she does not touch them until they are hers.

The Boy raises to four hundred.

The Foreman calls without thinking, the way a man calls when his hands hurt.

The Valuer folds sevens face down.

The Widow makes it eleven hundred.

The Steward looks at the pot and at the Boy and at his own four thousand two hundred, and calls. The Boy calls the raise without looking at it again. The Foreman calls the way he called the first time.

Four players. Four thousand four hundred in the middle, less the house.

The flop comes queen of spades, jack of spades, four of diamonds.

The Widow’s thumb moves on the felt. She has ace and king of spades. She has the two best spades in the deck and needs one more.

The Boy has king and nine of hearts, and the flop has given him a straight draw that wants a ten and nothing else, and he has not sat still since the cards landed.

The Steward has two nines. He has an underpair on a board with a queen and a jack, and he knows exactly what that is worth, which is very little, and he also knows that the pot is bigger than his hand deserves.

The Widow bets fourteen hundred.

Time, says the dealer, since the Steward has been eleven seconds.

The Steward folds his nines face up, which nobody at this table does, and says nothing about it. The Valuer looks at them for a long moment.

The Boy raises to three thousand six hundred. The Foreman calls, which is all of it, and turns his glass a quarter turn and leaves it.

The Widow calls.

The turn is the nine of spades.

Two things happen inside two people. The Widow has made the ace-high flush, and there is exactly one hand in the deck that beats it, and it is two cards, and she has counted them the way she counts everything, and she moves nothing at all — she has been at ramps and in halls and in rooms with clerks and she has an entire trade in not moving. The Steward, who folded nines, has just watched the third nine arrive on the turn, and the fourth is in the Boy’s hand where he cannot see it, and he does not blink, and afterwards, in the corridor, he will lean his forehead against the cold of the door frame for four seconds and then go home.

The Widow checks.

The Boy bets two thousand five hundred, which is everything he has left in front of him, and says, “All in,” and says it too early, before the chips are across the line.

The Widow calls, and the dealer splits the pot without being asked, the Foreman having been all in since the flop, which is the only thing that happens all night that nobody has to pay for.

The river is the ten of diamonds.

The Boy makes his straight. King, queen, jack, ten, nine. He has been drawing to the ten since the flop and it has come on the last card and he is on his feet.

The dealer says, Cards up.

The Boy turns over the king and nine of hearts.

The Widow turns over the ace and the king of spades and the Boy sits back down. The Foreman turns over two fours, a set on the flop that never improved, and he does not seem surprised.

The dealer pushes the main pot to the Widow, and the side pot to the Widow, and takes the five per cent off the top with a curved plastic wand, and rounds down.

The Foreman laughs once and says something about the works and stands up and puts on the wrong coat. The Valuer corrects him, kindly, and walks him to the stairs, and comes back having missed a hand he was owed a look at.

The Boy has nothing in front of him and he is not going home. He says the thing they all say, which is: the house books it.

The Valuer looks at the Widow. It is her pot; it is her call. The house will book a young man’s debt in words of proven retention at the board rate, less a handling charge, and there is a slip for it in the drawer under the green cloth, and everybody in Room Four knows which drawer.

“How many,” the Widow says.

“Four hundred and twenty at the spring board,” says the Valuer, who values things.

The Boy says, “I have got them.”

“You have got them,” she says. “You have not got four hundred of them. You have got two hundred and eleven and a mother.”

Nobody in Room Four has ever known what anybody has. She has counted his.

The Boy looks at his own hands on the cloth and touches them twice, as he touched the cards, and everybody at that table sees him do it and nobody says anything, since it is not a hand anymore.

“Sign it,” she says.

He signs it. The Valuer witnesses. The slip goes across the cloth face up and the Widow reads it twice, which is how she reads now, and folds it once, and puts it in her coat pocket, and the coat is on the rail, and she goes to the rail and puts the coat on with the slip inside it.

At the door she stops and looks back at the table, and the Boy is still sitting there with his hands flat on the green, and she is a woman with four months of her husband in her handbag and four hundred and twenty of a stranger’s words in her coat, and the room waits to hear what she will say, since the room has been waiting nine years to hear her say anything at all.

“Which one will you spend first,” the Boy says.

She looks at him for as long as it takes to be sure that he means it. Then she goes out, past the coats and the rail and the lots waiting in the dark for the morning, and does not answer, and the door swings twice.

The Steward waits three hands and then follows. In the corridor he leans his forehead against the door frame for four seconds. Then he goes home and does not tell his wife about the nines, and on Thursday he pays, and on the following Tuesday he brings a chair to a man at a ramp who has begun to explain about his mother, and does it in one motion, and says nothing at all about any of it.

The Valuer stays until five. He is the only one who has seen all four coats and knows what each is worth. He has, in his own way, had a good night: he folded sevens, he folded sixes, he watched a man show him a pair of nines on purpose, and he learned that the quiet one at the end counts other people’s words.

He puts the cloth back over the table. Underneath, the drawers are exactly where they were.
