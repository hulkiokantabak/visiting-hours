Room Four

Story 23

Listen

Synthetic narration · Download audio

The Widow is here for four months of her husband. Everybody knows. Nobody says it.

Room Four is used for viewings by day. At night a green cloth covers the valuing table. The drawer runner cuts across your forearm.

Five players. The dealer is house.

Blinds fifty and a hundred.

The Valuer has the button. He has valued three of the four coats on the rail.

The Steward is big blind. Nine years at this table. He has never missed a Thursday.

The Boy is twenty-three. Seven thousand two hundred. His wristband is bright. In the Tank he was moved toward somebody all evening and did not go.

The Widow is small blind. Eight thousand six hundred, counted twice. The month is the ninth. The room used to call her the widow. Now it calls her nothing.

The Foreman is off the far hill. Four thousand seven hundred of the site money left. His hands are wrecked.

The Boy looks once and touches his cards twice. The Steward knows he does that with every hand he likes. Never with a bluff.

The Boy raises to four hundred. The Foreman calls. The Valuer folds sevens face down.

The Widow makes it eleven hundred.

The Steward calls. The Boy calls without looking again. The Foreman calls.

Queen of spades. Jack of spades. Four of diamonds.

The Widow bets fourteen hundred.

Eleven seconds.

Time.

The Steward folds his nines face up. Nobody does that here. The Valuer studies them.

The Boy raises to three thousand six hundred. The Foreman calls with everything he has.

The Widow calls.

Nine of spades.

The Widow has the ace-high flush. She moves nothing.

The Steward watches the third nine arrive. He cannot see the fourth in the Boy's hand.

The Widow checks.

"All in," the Boy says before his last two thousand five hundred are across the line.

The Widow calls. The dealer splits the pots.

Ten of diamonds.

The Boy stands. His straight.

Cards up.

He turns over his king and nine of hearts.

The Widow turns over her ace and king of spades. He sits.

The Foreman shows two fours. Only a set.

Both pots go to the Widow. The dealer takes five percent with a curved plastic wand, to the second decimal, rounded down.

The Foreman laughs and puts on the wrong coat. The Valuer corrects him, kindly, walks him to the stairs and misses a hand.

The Boy has nothing. He is not going home.

"The house books it."

The Valuer looks at the Widow. Her pot. Her call. A young man's debt, booked in words of proven retention, less a handling charge. The slip is already in the drawer under the cloth.

"How many," she says.

"Four hundred and twenty at the spring board."

"I have got them."

"You have got them. You have not got four hundred of them. You have got two hundred and eleven and a mother."

Nobody here has ever known what anybody has. She has counted his.

The Boy looks at his hands and touches them twice.

"Sign it."

His forearm rests on the drawer runner. He leaves it there. His mouth is dry. The signature is childish, his mother's teaching still in the letters.

The Valuer witnesses. His handwriting is the better of the two. The Widow reads the slip twice, folds it once and puts her coat on.

At the door she looks back. Four months of her husband in her handbag. Four hundred and twenty of a stranger's words in her coat. The Boy's hands are still flat on the green.

"Which one will you spend first."

She looks at him until she is sure he means it. Then she goes. The door swings twice.

The Steward waits three hands. In the corridor he rests his forehead against the door frame for four seconds. He goes home and does not tell his wife about the nines.

On Thursday he pays. On the following Tuesday he brings a chair to a man at a ramp who has begun to explain about his mother. One motion.

The Valuer stays until five. He has seen all four coats now. Folded sevens. Folded sixes. Watched a man show him nines on purpose.

He puts the cloth back. Underneath, the drawers are exactly where they were.
