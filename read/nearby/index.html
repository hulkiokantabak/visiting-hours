TÜRKÇE

Nearby

story two

Listen · 4:31

He keeps a flat the way other men keep a razor. One room over the funicular’s middle station — a bed, a fork, a bicycle with good brakes. He has timed his exits. Any room in the city, under a minute, nothing left behind but heat in the sheets.

Nights he works the middle station. The funicular runs two cars on one cable, the up car and the down car balanced against each other; they meet at the loop outside his window, twelve seconds of lit glass level with lit glass, then apart. He checks tickets. He frees a jammed door with the flat of his hand. He passes a cellist her case through the gap and her fingers are all callus and no ring. The hill’s bodies ride through him all night — clerks asleep standing up, boys wet from the pools, a man who rides down every midnight and is never on the up car.

Sundays he swims in the sea until his shoulders burn, eats standing at the fish counter, sleeps alone with the window open. He would sign for it again.

In March the cellist rides up twice in one week and does not have her case with her either time. He is on the platform both times and both times says the thing he says, which is easy and warm and costs him nothing, and she goes up, and after that she takes the stair street instead. He has worked out why. He has not changed anything.

The service knows the hill. NEW NEARBY, it says at ten, and it is right — they are new, they are near. The lifeguard with chlorine still on her collarbones, who bites his shoulder and admires the mark like her own signature. The surgeon who sleeps holding his wrist, thumb on the pulse, and writes nothing down in the morning. The baker who is up at four and gone by five and leaves flour on him like weather. All of it is good. He is polite in the mornings and means it. You’re on a streak, the service says. The assistant suggests a warmer opening. He accepts the suggestion. Five stars, five stars, five stars. He sleeps best on the nights someone has just left.

In March there is a woman with cold hands who walks her thumb down his spine one bone at a time, counting under her breath like a woman going down stairs in the dark, and stops at the seventh. She presses, once. His whole back answers. He holds his breath. She notices the held breath and says nothing. Afterwards she dresses with her back to the window, her face to him. At the door she says, “You’re good at this, the way a hotel is good at it.” The door locks itself behind her.

The year turns and the service sends him his year. Eleven names, four and a half stars, a map of the hill with his nights on it like pins. He remembers nine bodies. Two of the names carry photographs taken in his room — his blanket, his window, the cable crossing the glass at the angle it only crosses from the bed — and he cannot put a face above the blanket in either one. In one photograph the pillowslips are a set he has never owned. He checks the cupboard. He owns them. He stops arguing with the year.

After that the mornings get loud. He buys a second plate. He goes back to the March thread and types his own sentence, spelled wrong, no suggestion accepted. This person is no longer nearby. He widens the radius by two hills. You have used your free introductions. He pays. The introductions arrive, warm as the middle setting. The surgeon comes back once and they lie afterwards side by side like two good reviews. Everyone answers warmly. The assistant offers three warmths and he takes the middle one. Nobody arrives.

On the last shift of the year the two cars meet at the loop and hold their twelve seconds. In the down car a hand comes up, palm to the glass. He raises his. He does not see whose it is. He is still holding his hand up when the cars have gone their ways, the cable ticking as it cools, and in his room above the station the second plate sits where it can be seen from the door.
