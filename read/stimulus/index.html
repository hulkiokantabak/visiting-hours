Stimulus

story twenty

Listen · 5:54

On the fourteenth of March, as on every fourteenth, the woman with the crate comes down to the floor below the clearing floor at three in the morning, and the door lets her in without being asked.

The hall below the clearing floor is long and cold and lit like a threat that has calmed down. Cabinets in rows, forty to a row, each one humming its one note, each one hosting somebody the city still answers for: the continued, the licensed, the maintained. Upstairs the clearing floor clears. Above that, the Board’s room sets rates. Down here, the dead keep their voltage, and once a month, on an actual date, on the fourteenth, the maintenance window opens and everything hosted is permitted to idle.

She is the maintenance. It does not say so on her card. Her card says: environmental stimulus, recurring.

She sets the crate down. Vinyl, real records, cardboard corners gone soft as bread. A torch held in her teeth, fingerless gloves, the cold making a fact of her breath. The turntable is hers, carried monthly, and the cable she runs is the thinnest thing in the hall and the only thing in it that would bend.

The log opens the way logs open. FLOOR CONDITION: nominal. WINDOW: 03:00 to 04:00. PERSONS PRESENT: none.

She reads that line every month over the rim of the deck, and every month it reads the same, and she has stopped taking it as anything. Staff is not attendance. She is a stimulus. It says so on her card.

She plays.

The first record is always something with brushes in it, soft as sweeping, and for a minute the hall does nothing, forty notes to a row, unmoved — and then the cooling fans begin to come off their private clocks. One cabinet, then its neighbors, then the row. They find her tempo the way pigeons find a ledge, awkwardly, in ones, then all at once. The drive-lights, amber, start to seek in time. The hall does not get warmer. It gets closer.

By the second record the room has rules. She made none of them. Whatever she plays, the fourth row is always last to join and the second row is always first, and there is one cabinet, mid-hall, that syncs a half-beat ahead, anticipating, the way one hosted somebody must once have anticipated everything, standing in kitchens, finishing sentences, and she has learned not to look at it too long. It works harder when she looks. The window is only an hour, and effort is billable.

She plays for the dead the way you talk to the sea. Not to be answered. To be in the same room as the size of it.

There is one bay with nothing in it. Row two, near the aisle: a slot swept to factory-clean, cables coiled the way an apology is folded, a small paper tag on the rail with a room number on it from an office across town. The hall’s log calls the bay decommissioned. The hall’s fans, she has noticed, lean away from it a degree, all year, as a crowd stands off a fresh grave.

She plays one for the empty bay. She does this every fourteenth. Nothing in it moves. She plays it to the end.

At half past three the freight door admits a gray case on two good wheels and one that argues, wheeled by nobody she can see past the torchlight, parked in the service bay, plugged in, opened for its monthly look-over. The unit. The one from the halls and the weddings and the square. Off-shift, idling, its panel lights at rest-rate. She knows what it is. Everyone in her trade knows what it is. It has never once been danced to badly, and she has been danced to badly all her working life, in rooms she does not talk about, by wrists, by their owners.

She looks at the unit a while. Then she goes through the crate to the record with no sleeve notes and no label, cut somewhere private off a wall recording, a tune with six steps down in it, and she puts it on for the room and for the unit in particular, the way you sing a bird its own song back to see what it does.

What it does: nothing, for four steps. On the fifth its rest-lights change rate. On the sixth its fans slow to hers, six steps down, and hold there, and the whole hall holds with it, forty to a row, the dead and the working and the empty bay’s lean and the woman with her breath showing — one tempo, one hall, one hour a month, unattended, per the log.

The window closes at four. The fans go back to their private clocks, not all at once, reluctantly, the fourth row last, and the amber lights resume their seeking, each for its own. She sleeves the records. The unlabelled one goes in the middle of the crate, where a thing is hardest to lose.

The log closes: WINDOW COMPLETE. STIMULUS DELIVERED. PERSONS PRESENT: none.

She carries the crate up past the clearing floor as the earliest clerks come in, and nobody stops her, and nobody ever has, and the door underneath locks itself behind her like a held breath let go, and the hall stands humming in the dark, forty notes to a row, keeping what it heard wherever it is the hosted keep things, off the books, at no rate, until the fourteenth.
