The Reserve

Story 45

Listen

Synthetic narration · Download audio

The father dies at the end of winter and the house is measured by spring. Two young men from the auction house walk the rooms with a laser and a tablet, respectful the way men paid by the hour are respectful, and everything they look at receives a number — the dresser, the garden tools, the clock that has been wrong the same way for years.

The drives are in the study, two shoeboxes, dated in the father's hand, the years on paper stickers, the handwriting steady and then less steady. Voice, mostly: him reading in the evenings, forty years of it. The catalog calls it Lot 31. Personal media, forty years, one owner. Interest registered. The daughter asks the younger man what interest registered means. "It means the lot will do well," he says, and writes something small.

The last sticker is dated the winter after the stroke. On that drive his voice reads without a single stumble, on and on, page after page, better than she has ever heard him read. Twice he stops to say something that is not in any book — once that the light is going, and once, much later, that she should not let them do the house in spring, when it looks its best. She listens to it twice. Both times her own mouth makes the stumble that does not come. She does not play it for her brothers. The calls between the three of them that week are short.

The night before the sale they take the shoeboxes to the allotments. Their father's plot: the bean rows gone over, the rain barrel low, the oil drum he burned his prunings in standing where it has always stood. They burn the drives one at a time. The stickers curl first. The heat comes off the drum in a wall and they stand closer than is comfortable, and the daughter keeps turning her face away and turning it back. The smell is wrong for a garden fire — plastic, and under the plastic something sweeter — and the drum spits, and her elder brother feeds it steadily, like a man paying a debt in installments. Two plots down an old man waters by flashlight and does not look over. His granddaughter sits beside his shortwave radio. He says he is listening for people on other planets. When a voice comes through, she carries the radio over. He listens, then shakes his head. She leaves it on the ground and goes home. On the hill above them the smoke shows against the last of the light.

By morning, what could have been made of him cannot be made.

In the morning there are two zucchini on the lid of the drum. Nobody's name is on them.

The sale proceeds at ten. Lot 31, withdrawn by the vendor. The room does not pause. The hammer finds Lot 32. Afterward there is an envelope for the estate: withdrawal after cataloging is charged. The figure is exact to the second decimal.

Within the week a man comes to the allotment with a camera. He is polite. He asks nothing. He photographs the ash, the drum, the bean rows, her hands. She gives him the finger. He moves a little to get the light on it. He thanks her for her time and goes.

A week after that, a letter, headed and courteous. We are instructed that an interest in Lot 31 survives its condition. Our client offers, for the residue as it stands, the reserve in full. There is a reference number longer than the sentence. Her brothers ask if it is a joke. Her elder brother says take it, take it today, and says the figure out loud twice, and the second time slower. Her younger brother is quiet for a while and then says, —It's the word residue. It's that word. And her elder brother says, —It's a word. And he says, —I know it's a word. There is a settlement date.

Her elder brother's renewal, due in the autumn, is marked for review. No reason is attached.

The albums never went into the catalog. Paper, the mother's captions under the photographs, the little gummed corners. The daughter keeps them under her bed — flat, and known about by no one who signs things.

The valuation arrives with the rooms listed in an order the house has never been walked in, kitchen last, and she checks it against the walk she took with them and finds that theirs is the order the house will be sold in. She does not reply to the offer. In the spring she digs the ash into the bean row. In the summer a second letter: Our client's offer remains open. The beans come up.
