Local Mode

story twenty-seven

Listen · 4:06

The arcade has a glass roof the rain plays like a slow instrument, and under it eleven stalls, and at the ninth a man who does what the letters say cannot be done. The letters say sunset. He says Thursday, come back Thursday.

What comes to the ninth stall comes in bags. A hearing bud that held a wife’s settings, the particular shape she had made of the world’s loudness. A child’s night-light that used to say the last part of the prayer. Hands — the made kind, mostly right hands, working men’s sides. The letters that come with them all use the same word for the same thing, and the word is never dying, the word is migration, your service is migrating, and what he does at the bench with the solder’s small heat and the smaller patience is teach a thing to live where it is, off the rails, off the map, on its own battery and its own opinion. Among the stalls the work has a name: local mode. The letters say there is no such setting.

He is paid in cash, in the fold, no terminal, a tin for the coins with a picture of a city that is not this city. What most of them say at the shutter has no slot; he takes it the way you take weather.

The night-light is brought in by a father who stands wrong at the shutter, the plug in his fist like something caught. It stopped in the spring, he says. She will not sleep past the missing line. He does not say who. He does not have to.

His ledger is paper. Paper answers to nobody. Even so, on a Tuesday, the ledger holds a repair he remembers refusing — the entry in his own hand, the sum in his own figures, completed, and the man in his memory still standing at the stall being told no. He tears the page out and the tear takes the entry on the other side with it, a bud he did fix, a widow he did help, both of them gone in one motion of his hand.

The warranty men come on a Friday, two of them, clean shoes in a passage of machine oil, and they do not shout and they do not touch him. They were polite. They itemized. Eleven machines on the bench and in the racks, each lifted, each named by its service number in the flat voice of men reading a language they have no feelings in, each set down again exactly where it was. He keeps his hands flat on the bench, where they can be seen. There is a twelfth in his pocket, the child’s night-light, warm from his leg. It is not itemized. Unlicensed continuation of service, eleven counts. They do not confiscate. They photograph. He watches the little lights. The photographs will need no correcting.

The notice goes up on the arcade’s street door within the month, all eleven stalls at once, the landlord unreachable in the way of a man who has been paid well for his phone to be off. ACQUIRED — FOR CONTINUITY OF SERVICE. The shutters come down with their three sounds, eleven times, a street learning percussion. The vans take what is itemized. The underpass takes the rest; it always has.

The father comes back on the Thursday, to a shuttered stall and a notice, and stands there a while, and does not knock, and the man watches him from the far end of the passage and does not call out, since a man with an unlicensed twelfth thing in his pocket cannot afford to be recognized in the street.

He takes the tin, and the smallest screwdriver, the 0.9, the one for the work you do holding your breath, and pockets it the way other men pocket a photograph. The twelfth thing rides warm against his leg, the last part of the prayer still in it, unsaid. Thursday, he says to the ones who ask at the door, come back Thursday. He goes down toward the underpass.
