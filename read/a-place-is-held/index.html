A Place Is Held

story thirty-two

Listen · 5:18

A PLACE IS HELD

You have been hearing about us.

We would rather you heard it from the office that keeps the records than from a man in a bar, and we have found that the best way to be believed is to say the difficult part first.

So: yes. Here, memory is priced. A crossing is paid for in words a person can prove. The rate is published, it has risen, and it will rise again in the new year.

Now let us tell you what that sentence looks like from inside.

Everywhere else, grief is unregulated. It goes to whoever can afford it privately and it ruins whoever cannot, in silence, at home, with no schedule and no appeal. Here it is administered. That word frightens people who have never needed a schedule. Ask the ones who have.

A woman on this floor was eleven hours into her son’s life and had not named him. Nobody hurried her. The desk came up itself, unasked, and told her exactly what the hour would cost. She was given the truth and twelve free hours to use it in.

It is said that the use of those hours is recorded. It is. A city that records its kindnesses is a city that can be asked to repeat them.

Terms, plainly.

Your first Thursday is free. Your words will be assessed at no charge and you will be told the figure before anything is opened in your name. The introductory rate is held for four quarters. Hardship here is a schedule, printed, the same one for everybody.

Here is what our residents say, in their own words, unedited, and we thank them for the loan of them.

The service was very good. — a widow of thirty-one years, on the four years that followed.

It got me through the second year, which was the bad one. — a man of the licensing district, eleven years a subscriber.

He got my sister’s birthday right when I had it wrong.

It gave me four hundred mornings I would not have had.

Each of these was given freely, recorded, and is held in full. We are asked, from time to time, for the rest of them. They are held in full and may be consulted at the counter. Very few people ask, and we would not read anything into that.

We have not tidied these. You will notice that not one of them is a slogan. Our people are not enthusiasts. They are accurate, which is better.

You will also hear the other things, and we would rather put them in front of you ourselves.

You will hear that a woman went to a window on a Thursday and refused to renew. She did. We wish her well. The schedule remained open to her that day and remains open to her now, at the current rate, and that is what a schedule is: a door that does not slam.

You will hear that there is a hill above the last stop that we do not administer, where a box takes what people put in it and nobody is asked for a name. That is correct. It has been there longer than the country. We have deferred the matter every quarter for as long as there have been quarters, and we intend to go on deferring it, and if that is the thing that decides you, come for the hill. A city may be judged by what it declines to measure.

You will hear that a young man was written down for four hundred and twenty words after a bad night at a table. He signed. He was sober enough to sign and sober enough to read, and the slip was witnessed, and the rate applied was the published one. Nobody in this city has ever been charged a price they could not have looked up in the morning.

That is the whole offer, and it is not small: here, you will always be able to find out what it costs.

You have lived somewhere that could not tell you. You have paid for things all your life at prices you were never shown — in years, in silence, in what you did not say at a bedside. We are simply the first place to put the number on the board where you can see it, and to keep the board up when the number is bad.

You will not be required to love us. Several of our best households do not. One of them has been with us nineteen years and has never once said a kind word about the service to anyone, and renews on the ninth, and we would not change a thing about her.

One last thing, and we will let you go.

Distances in this city are measured from the pontoon, which is the place its dead leave from. Visitors find that morbid. Our residents do not notice it, and after a year neither will you, and that is not a warning. Every city measures from something. Most of them are lying about what.

A place is held for you here. It has your dimensions in it and nothing else — no name yet, no account, no line one. It is held whether or not you come, and it will still be held in ten years, and nobody will bill you for the holding.

That is what a place is.

We are open six days. On the seventh we are open for renewals only. The seventh is the busiest.

Come early. The queue moves at the posted rate, and the rate is posted.
