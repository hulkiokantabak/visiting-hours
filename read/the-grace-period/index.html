TÜRKÇE

The Grace Period

story three

Listen · 3:54

The license desk on the maternity floor does not close. Birth does not, so the desk does not. The corridor is warm at the ceiling and cold at the ankle, and the women who have been up all night stand in the warm part with their arms crossed, holding on to what heat they came in with.

The first hours are free. It is on the wall in the kind of type that means a kindness has been costed: THE GRACE PERIOD, TWELVE HOURS FROM THE FIRST BREATH, DURING WHICH NO CHARGE ACCRUES.

The midwife has worked nights nineteen years and can tell in a minute which mothers will name early. The early ones name in the room, wet-haired, triumphant, the tag clipped before the tea has cooled — a name goes on the roll, the roll goes to the desk, the desk opens an account, and the account runs: cot, warmth, one reading a day, all the way out to a ramp nobody here is thinking about. In the private wing tonight a woman names hers in four minutes. She has money.

The woman in the third bed is eleven hours in and has not named her son.

She holds him with both arms, which nobody had to tell her, and has not put him down since the ward went quiet. The midwife brings tea twice, and the second time says the thing she is not supposed to say: unnamed at twelve hours is not nothing. The file goes from grace to pending.

“I know what pending is,” the woman says. “My brother’s pending. He’s forty-one. He has never stood in a queue in his life and he has never been served in one either.”

At half past four the man from the desk comes up himself. He does not hurry her. He explains, correctly and at no charge, that a name entered before the hour is the cheapest name she will ever enter, that the fee doubles at twelve hours and again at a week, and that there is a hardship schedule; and he prints the hardship schedule and leaves it on the blanket, and does not once look at the clock she is looking at.

At eleven hours forty she says a word to the baby. Nineteen years of first words said into new ears, and the midwife knows this one is not a name.

“Is that the name?”

“No,” the woman says. “That one’s for later.”

She says it again into the small ear, the way you say a thing you want kept: once, clearly, the whole word.

“And the name?”

She looks at the wall, where the type is still kind. “Write it down,” she says, and gives him one, and it is short, and the midwife has never heard it in this city.

The midwife signs the floor’s copy as witness, which is her name beside the account for as long as the account exists.

The account opens at 04:52. Cot. Warmth. A reading, at the desk’s convenience. The tag prints and closes around the ankle with a click that everyone on the floor learns to hear.

The fourth line is not a charge. It is a note: the registration was made at eleven hours forty. The note is not removable. It will be on the file at the school, and at the licensing hall, and at the ramp. The grace period is free. The use of it is recorded.

He is eleven hours old and eight minutes registered and he has a word in him that nobody has been paid for.

At six the midwife takes the night’s linen down. It is raining on the far hill, purchased, out of season. On the stairs she stops at the window nobody cleans, and looks up at the monastery, and does the arithmetic she does every night on the way back.
