Visiting Hours

Listen

Synthetic narration

The funicular's last stop is called the last stop. The hill continues without it. The steps begin at a rusted gate, propped open longer than anyone has been alive to prop it. They have been counted many times and never twice to the same number. She starts up in the early cool with the return half of her ticket in her pocket and water in a bottle and no reason she has said aloud. The steps are stone, then older stone, then stone that has given up being anything but hill. A rope rail runs beside them, polished to glass where hands have needed it, slack where they have not. In a niche the size of a loaf, wax stands on old wax, still warm at the wick, though she has met no one coming down. Her phone loses the city at the treeline and stays lost, a stone that used to sing. She puts it away. After the treeline the sounds peel away in the order they were added — the deliveries first, then the traffic, then the harbor bell, then the channel's whole patient business — until the loudest thing on the path is her own blood. The air changes hands somewhere in the last hundred steps. At the top the wall is low and white, mended in every mortar fashion there has ever been, and the gate stands open.

The monastery is small, whitewash and smoke-black, a courtyard the size of a sail, rooms swept to the texture of paper. On the step sits a narrow gray cat, unhurried, one ear notched by something settled long ago. The cat considers her, decides she is expected, and says so. On a stand by the door lies the visitors' book, open, unattended, the hands in it drying centuries apart and the ink all one age. She does not sign yet. She has been up nine minutes. She has stopped checking the time.

There are others. A woman in mourning, three hundred years out of fashion, lights her wax from the standing wax, exact, practiced. A child asks her, in a dialect the city has stopped losing sleep over, whether the boats still go out in pairs; she says yes, and the child is satisfied, and that is the whole transaction. Two men stand at the wall looking down at the city, each between his wars, and one of the wars has not happened yet. A goat looks at her over the wall and asks if the mule track has reopened; she says there is a funicular now; the goat withdraws, unconvinced.

The priest finds her at the box. He is younger than the building in the way everyone is. The state funds them, he says — continuity of heritage; the debit goes out monthly, they are told. The box is separate. "The box is not for us. It lets you feel you've given something to a place that needs nothing." She puts a coin in anyway. "Everyone does," he says. "That is the feeling working." She asks how old the house is. Two thousand years, he says, or three; they are open toward both ends, and the paperwork is older than paper. In a side room a man who says he has no faith is asleep on a prayer mat. Someone has covered his feet.

Toward afternoon a visitor whose coat closes with no fastening she can find takes her hand and thanks her. For the water, on the steps, next summer or the one after. She has no answer, and none is waited for. Her mouth has been dry since the treeline. She has not opened the bottle. Later, alone, she looks into the box. Among the morning's coins lies one she knows the way she knows her own handwriting, worn smooth, older than the country. She closes the lid.

The smuggler comes up at dusk, still drying, salt in his seams, a stain at his side that has stopped mattering. The book does not offer him a line. They struck him from the register at the water, he tells her, before the other thing. The crossing will never have him now, in any words. So he came up. He looks at her the way the shot look at the standing. "I had to exist to see it. It never had to see me." He stops, and starts again. "That's not — I had to be there. It didn't." She has nothing. She unscrews the cap and holds the bottle out. The cat, from the step, without opening her eyes: "The hill watched the city arrive. It is prepared to watch it leave."

In the evening she means to start down while the light holds. The gate stands open; it has stood open all day; nobody has used it downhill. Below her the city lights itself window by window and the channel keeps its arithmetic. The funicular's two cars rise and fall against each other and meet — twelve seconds of lit glass — and part. By the door the bell has a cord. It has not been rung today; the cord is worn smooth all the same. The bell rings the hour and the cat calls it, in case. The return half of her ticket has gone soft in her pocket. She goes back in. Passing the stand, she signs the book at last; by the time she reaches the candles the ink is the age of all the rest. The gate stays open. It is visiting hours; it is always visiting hours.
