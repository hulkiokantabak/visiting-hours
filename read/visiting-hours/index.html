Visiting Hours

Listen · 5:58

The funicular’s last stop is called the last stop, and the hill continues without it. The steps begin at a rusted gate that has been propped open longer than anyone has been alive to prop it, and they have been counted many times and never twice to the same number. She starts up in the early cool with the return half of her ticket in her pocket and water in a bottle and no reason she has said aloud. The steps are stone, then older stone, then stone that has given up being anything but hill. A rope rail runs beside them, polished to glass where hands have needed it, slack where they have not, and there are benches at the intervals people of every century have needed them. In a niche the size of a loaf, wax stands on old wax, a spring of it, still warm at the wick when she passes, though she has met no one coming down. Her phone loses the city at the treeline and stays lost, a stone that used to sing. After the treeline the sounds peel away in the order they were added — the deliveries first, then the traffic, then the harbor bell, then the channel’s whole patient business — until the loudest thing on the path is her own blood. The air changes hands somewhere in the last hundred steps. She does not notice the border, only the new owner. At the top the wall is low and white, mended in every mortar fashion there has ever been, and the gate stands open.

The monastery is small, whitewash and smoke-black, a courtyard the size of a sail, rooms swept to the texture of paper. On the step sits a cat, narrow, gray, unhurried, with one ear notched by something that was settled a long time ago. The cat considers her, decides she is expected, and says so. On a stand by the door lies the visitors’ book, open, unattended, the hands in it drying centuries apart and the ink all one age. She does not sign yet. She has been up nine minutes and has already stopped checking the time.

There are others; there are always others in a place with no schedule. A woman in mourning, dressed three hundred years out of fashion, lights her wax from the standing wax, exact, practiced. A child asks her, in a dialect the city has stopped losing sleep over, whether the boats still go out in pairs; she says yes, and the child is satisfied, and that is the whole transaction. Two men stand at the wall looking down at the city, each between his wars, and one of the wars has not happened yet. A goat looks at her over the wall and asks if the mule track has reopened; she says there is a funicular now; the goat withdraws, unconvinced.

The priest finds her at the box. He is younger than the building in the way everyone is, and he has the manner of a man who answers the same three questions all day. The state funds them, he says — continuity of heritage; the debit goes out monthly, they are told. The box is separate. “The box is not for us. It is so you can feel you have given something to a place that needs nothing.” She puts a coin in anyway. “Everyone does,” he says, approving. “That is the feeling working.” She asks how old the house is. Two thousand years, he says, or three; they are open toward both ends, and the paperwork is older than paper.

Toward afternoon a visitor whose coat closes with no fastening she can find takes her hand and thanks her — for the water, on the steps, next summer or the one after; she will know the summer when she is in it. She has no answer, and none is waited for. Later, alone, she looks into the box. Among the morning’s coins lies one she knows the way she knows her own handwriting, worn smooth, older than the country. She closes the lid.

The smuggler comes up at dusk, still drying, salt in his seams, a stain at his side that has stopped mattering, walking like a man whose name has stopped weighing anything. The book does not offer him a line. They struck him from the register at the water, he tells her, before the other thing; the crossing will never have him now, at any rate, in any words. He says the phrase the way a man says a price. So he came up. He looks at her the way the shot look at the standing. “I had to exist to see it. It never had to see me.” He stops, and starts again. “That’s not — I had to be there. It didn’t.” She has nothing. The cat, from the step, without opening her eyes: “The hill watched the city arrive. It is prepared to watch it leave.”

In the evening she means to start down while the light holds. The gate stands open; it has stood open all day; nobody has used it downhill. Below her the city lights itself window by window, the channel keeps its arithmetic, and the funicular’s two cars rise and fall against each other and meet — twelve seconds of lit glass — and part. By the door the bell has a cord, for the hours when nobody is at the gate. It has not been rung today; the cord is worn smooth all the same. The bell rings the hour and the cat calls it, in case. The return half of her ticket has gone soft in her pocket, the way paper does when it has been carried past its journey. She goes back in, and passing the stand she signs the book at last; by the time she reaches the candles the ink is the age of all the rest. The gate stays open. It is visiting hours; it is always visiting hours.
