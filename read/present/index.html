Present

story nine

Listen · 2:19

The register is read by the room at nine, wet coats on the hooks, names in seat order, and at the fourth name there is the pause a child takes to stop whispering, and then, from the seat by the window, in the standard voice, with the standard brightness: Present. The desk fee sits on the term’s invoice between milk and swimming. The card renews on a Thursday.

A boy in the second row asks, in the first week, whether she will get older too. He is told that the desk is the same desk and the register is the same register, which is not an answer, and he takes it for one. The children are quicker about it than the staff. They hide things in her desk, the desk nobody’s mother turns out, and she thanks each of them by name, and at games she waits against the painted lines, hands folded, eight years old, exact, as printed. The class photograph is taken in autumn; by spring it has her in the middle row, and the children who stood either side have moved a little apart without remembering moving.

The renewals stop the year the class learns long division. The head keeps the name on the roll one term more, unpaid, and the term’s headcount holds, and the funding that follows the headcount holds with it, and nobody asks which kindness it was. The teacher who reads the register asks once, in the corridor, whether the fourth name might be taken off now, and is told it will sort itself out at the end of term, and reads it every morning until it does. She leaves the school the following year and does not say why, and then the fourth name is a different name and the desk holds pencil shavings and somebody’s marbles like any desk. At the reunion, twenty years on, somebody finds the old paper register — the paper one, the one the funding never read — and reads it out for the joke of it, and the room answers Present in its grown voices, name after name, and at the fourth name the room holds, and a woman who was the girl at the next desk finds she has put her hand flat on the table, which she did not decide to do. The silence that answers is eight years old.
